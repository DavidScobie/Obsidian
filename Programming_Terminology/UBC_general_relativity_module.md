General relativity is essential for the accuracy of GPS tracking because it describes how gravity affects time, which is crucial for precise positioning. GPS satellites orbit Earth at high altitudes, experiencing a weaker gravitational field than on the surface. This means their onboard atomic clocks tick slightly faster than clocks on Earth. Without accounting for this difference, GPS would quickly become inaccurate.

- **Time Dilation:**
    
    Einstein's theory of General Relativity predicts that time passes slower in stronger gravitational fields and faster in weaker ones. 
    
- **GPS Satellite Clocks:**
    
    GPS satellites carry extremely accurate atomic clocks. These clocks are affected by both General Relativity (due to their altitude and weaker gravity) and Special Relativity (due to their orbital velocity). 
    
- **General Relativity's Effect:**
    
    Because GPS satellites orbit high above the Earth (approximately 20,000 km), the gravitational field is weaker than on the Earth's surface. This means the satellite clocks experience time dilation, ticking faster than clocks on Earth. 
    
- **Special Relativity's Effect:**
    
    The satellites' high speed (around 14,000 km/h) also causes a time dilation effect, but in the opposite direction, making their clocks tick slower. 
    
- **Combined Effects:**
    
    The net effect is that satellite clocks gain about 38 microseconds per day relative to clocks on Earth. 
    
- **Accuracy Implications:**
    
    If these relativistic effects were not accounted for, the GPS system would accumulate errors of about 11 kilometers per day, making it unusable for accurate navigation. 
    
- **Correcting for Relativity:**
    
    GPS receivers must compensate for these relativistic effects to accurately calculate their position using the time signals received from the satellites.
